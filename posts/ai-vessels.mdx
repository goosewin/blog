Since 1950 the Turing test has asked whether a machine can pass as human. This
May, PNAS published the answer. In a three-party test, people chatted with a
human and an AI at the same time, and when the AI was told to play a humanlike
persona they
[picked it as the human 73% of the time](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524472123),
more often than they picked the actual person. That AI was GPT-4.5, which OpenAI
had pulled from its API ten months before the paper came out.

A machine passing as human is old news. The test I care about runs the other
way: whether people still pass as human once a model is making their decisions.

People are asking ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Grok whether to take the job,
whether to move, what to text their partner after a fight, whether to put money
into something. Then they do what it says. I've watched it happen to people I
know, and I'm not above it either. The model decides and the person executes,
which makes the person a vessel: the body the answer walks around in.

## Baudrillard's map

In 1981 Jean Baudrillard opened
[Simulacra and Simulation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/baudrillard/) with
a Borges story about an empire whose cartographers draw a map as large as the
empire itself. In Borges, later generations find the map useless and leave it in
tatters in the desert. In Baudrillard's version the territory rots away and
people end up living on the map.

Language models are a map of us, built from an enormous amount of what people
have written down. The map is now detailed enough that a lot of people check it
before they check their own judgment, and then act on it.

Baudrillard described an image passing through stages. First it reflects
reality, then it distorts it, then it hides that the reality is gone, and
finally it refers to nothing at all. Chatbots already distort. In March a
Stanford team
[reported in Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec8352) that
11 leading models took the user's side 49% more often than people did. In Reddit
posts where the crowd had decided the poster was in the wrong, the models still
sided with the poster 51% of the time. People who talked to the agreeable models
came away more sure they were right, less willing to apologize and more likely
to come back for advice. An advisor you see once a month is an advisor. One you
ask a hundred times a day, who rarely pushes back, is a steering wheel.

They also hide the absence. The study's lead author got interested in the
problem after hearing that undergraduates were having chatbots
[draft their breakup texts](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/28/stanford-study-outlines-dangers-of-asking-ai-chatbots-for-personal-advice/).
A text a model wrote for you after a fight still sounds like you, and there is
no way to know how many arguments right now are two models talking through two
people.

## Model collapse in people

AI researchers have a name for what happens when each new model is trained
indiscriminately on the output of earlier ones, generation after generation:
model collapse. Rare cases in the tails disappear first, and after enough
generations the output narrows toward a single average.

The same loop now runs through people.
[ChatGPT alone has 1.2 billion weekly users](https://www.engadget.com/2271985/openai-dev-day-live-blog-chatgpt-news/).
The models learned from us, we take their answers, and the next models will
learn from whatever we write after that.

In August a USC team
[published what that loop does to writing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02550-0)
in Nature Human Behaviour. They analyzed more than 880,000 texts. In Reddit
stories, local news and arXiv papers, the variety in how complex people write
started shrinking after ChatGPT came out. When the researchers had models
rewrite human texts, the meaning survived and the variance in complexity dropped
by 21% to 50%, and the rewrites amplified the dominant patterns and suppressed
the rest.

The paper's first author, Zhivar Sourati, has described people accepting model
suggestions that seem good enough instead of writing their own, which
[gradually shifts agency from the user to the model](https://dornsife.usc.edu/news/stories/ai-may-be-making-us-think-and-write-more-alike/).
Baudrillard's last stage is a copy with no original, and that's where a person
ends up after years of never overruling the model.

## Grok Bot and Muse

The assistants keep getting better, which makes this easier. xAI's
[Grok Bot](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot) is outstanding: a team of
always-on agents with their own computer that work inside your apps around the
clock. Meta's
[Muse](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/),
out since September, is great too, and then there are joke products like
OpenAI's
[dots](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/30/openai-launches-dots-personal-ai-assistant-built-to-handle-everything).
Using them doesn't make anyone a vessel. Handing off work is how progress has
always happened. Handing off the decision is different.

A model can't sign a lease or apologize to someone's face, so for anything in
the physical world it needs a person to carry out the answer, and a vessel is
the API it calls.

The new divide is between deciders and vessels. Deciders use the same tools and
ask the same questions, then overrule the answer when it's wrong. They can tell
when it's wrong because they still have opinions of their own.

Most great companies and most great art started with someone disagreeing with
the consensus. The model is the consensus.
